# What a Constellation Actually Is

A **constellation** is a coordinated set of satellites designed to provide a service together — for LEO broadband, that service is continuous connectivity. This module turns “lots of satellites” into **structure**: shells, planes, inclination, and phasing.

## From one orbit to a net

Recall ESA’s point: LEO communications satellites typically work as a **constellation** forming a coverage net (<InlineSource id="esaOrbits" />). Designers choose:

- **how high** (altitude / shell),  
- **how the orbit is tilted** (inclination),  
- **how many distinct orbit rings** (planes),  
- **how many satellites share each ring**,  
- and **how they are spaced in time** (phasing).

Change those knobs and you change gaps, handovers, polar coverage, and collision geometry.

## Key terms (learn these cold)

### Orbital plane
Imagine a flat disk slicing through Earth. A satellite’s path lies in that disk (approximately). Multiple satellites can share one plane, chasing each other around the same ring.

### Inclination
The tilt of the plane relative to the equator.  
- ~**53°** class inclinations (common in Starlink broadband shells) favor mid-latitudes.  
- Higher inclinations / near-polar planes help high latitudes.  
Public Starlink shell tables list inclinations such as 43°, 53°, 70°, and ~97° sun-synchronous-class planes depending on shell (<InlineSource id="starlinkAltitudes" />).

### Shell
A **shell** is a population of satellites at a common design altitude (and usually a design inclination pattern) intended as a long-term home while providing service. Starlink’s space-safety documentation defines shells as passively deconflicted orbit sets at common altitude and inclination (<InlineSource id="starlinkAltitudes" />).

Multiple shells = layers of the net (different altitudes / missions).

### Phasing
Where each satellite sits *along* its track relative to neighbors. Even spacing reduces coverage holes and clustering.

### RAAN (advanced vocabulary)
**Right ascension of the ascending node** is the orientation of the plane around Earth — essentially how the plane is rotated when viewed from above. Spreading planes in RAAN is how you wrap coverage around longitudes.

## Walker-style intuition (not a claim about proprietary layouts)

Satellite engineering often discusses **Walker-type** constellations: a structured pattern of planes and phases that tiles coverage evenly. You do not need the full notation. Remember the idea:

> Pick a symmetric pattern so that as Earth rotates and satellites move, users rarely see “empty sky.”

Our lab uses a simplified even-plane / even-phase model for teaching — **not** a reverse-engineered Starlink configuration.

## Starlink as a multi-shell case study (public data)

As of Starlink’s public constellation-altitude documentation (2026 planning horizon), broadband and other variants occupy **distinct altitude bands**, for example:

- V1/V2 broadband shells planned in roughly the **450–490 km** range (with several inclinations),  
- lower bands (~**330–370 km**) associated with direct-to-cell and future V3 broadband planning,  

with ongoing **shell lowering** from earlier higher operational altitudes for safety reasons (<InlineSource id="starlinkAltitudes" />).

Exact live counts and which objects are truly operational change continuously. Independent catalogs — notably <InlineSource id="mcdowellStarlink">Jonathan McDowell’s Starlink statistics</InlineSource> — are the standard public reality check used by journalists and researchers.

Regulatory filings (FCC authorizations and modifications) are the legal design envelope; operational reality evolves inside (and sometimes by amending) that envelope (<InlineSource id="fccStarlink" />).

</Callout>

## Coverage is geometry + density

In the lab:

- Too few planes or sats → **gaps** (offline stretches).  
- Higher density → fewer gaps, **more handovers**.  
- Inclination and user latitude interact (high-latitude users care about polar-ish planes).

## Collision awareness (systems view)

Dense shells require disciplined spacing, autonomous maneuvering, and altitude choices that deconflict with other operators and crewed stations. Starlink’s public materials discuss altitude selection partly as a **safety deconfliction** lever (<InlineSource id="starlinkAltitudes" />). Module 4’s station-keeping is what keeps the designed pattern real.

## What to carry forward

You can now read a constellation as:

**shells × planes × sats/plane × inclination × phasing → coverage map over time**

<Advanced title="Optional: why multiple shells?">
Different altitudes trade latency, footprint size, debris decay time, and capacity density. Different inclinations target different latitude bands. Separating missions (broadband vs direct-to-cell) into bands can also reduce operational coupling. Multi-shell design is product architecture, not just “more dots.”
</Advanced>
