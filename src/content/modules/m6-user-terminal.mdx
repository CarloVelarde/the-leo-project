## The user terminal is not a GEO dish

Classic GEO terminals often use a mechanically pointed parabolic dish aimed at a fixed sky location. LEO satellites **move**. Continuously slewing a heavy dish for consumer broadband is awkward, slow, and failure-prone.

Starlink’s public technology materials highlight **phased-array antennas** on both user terminals and satellites — antennas that can steer beams **electronically** (<InlineSource id="starlinkTech" />).

## Phased arrays in plain language

A phased array is many small antenna elements acting together. By adjusting the **phase** (timing) of the signal at each element, the array can reinforce energy in one direction and cancel it in others — steering a beam without physically rotating a big dish.

Intuition:

- **Mechanical dish:** turn the hardware to point.  
- **Phased array:** retune the electronics to point.

That enables a low-profile “pizza box” terminal (often nicknamed “Dishy” in public discussion) to track LEO satellites across the sky.

## Establishing a link (mental model)

At a high level, a session looks like:

1. **Discover** candidates: which satellites are above a usable **elevation** angle?  
2. **Select** a serving satellite (and possibly prepare a next one).  
3. **Point beams** both ways (user terminal and satellite payloads are both sophisticated).  
4. **Authenticate / join** the network (details vary; treat as “become a network peer”).  
5. **Carry packets** while geometry remains good.  
6. **Hand over** before the link dies.

### Elevation matters

Near the horizon, paths through the atmosphere are longer, blockage is more likely (trees, buildings), and antennas may perform worse. Networks define a **minimum elevation** — below it, a satellite is treated as unusable. Our lab uses a geometric elevation mask as a teaching model.

## Handovers (handoffs)

Because LEO satellites set in minutes, the terminal must switch **serving satellites** regularly. A good system aims for **make-before-break** behavior: prepare the next link before dropping the current one so applications see continuity.

In the lab:

- **Online / offline** ≈ “is anyone above min elevation?”  
- **Handoff counter** ≈ serving-sat identity changes.  
- **Elevation / range** on the insight panel show the geometric quality of the current link.

Real networks also juggle load, interference, and gateway/ISL path quality — geometry is necessary but not sufficient.

## Why this feels like cellular — and why it isn’t

Mobile phones hand over between towers as you drive. LEO handovers are similar *in spirit* (change attachment point while moving relative geometry), but:

- the “towers” move at ~7.5+ km/s,  
- the cell footprints sweep across Earth,  
- and the backhaul may go through space lasers or distant gateways (Module 7).

## What to carry forward

User experience = **constellation geometry** + **antenna agility** + **handover policy**. Module 7 zooms out: once packets leave your terminal, where do they go?

<Advanced title="Optional: Doppler and tracking">
Relative motion creates Doppler shift in radio frequencies and requires continuous tracking updates. Phased arrays and modem design absorb this; it is one reason LEO user links are software-defined and compute-heavy compared with static GEO pointing.
</Advanced>
