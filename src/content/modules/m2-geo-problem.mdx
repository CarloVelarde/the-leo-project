# Why Ordinary Satellite Internet Felt Slow

Module 1 gave you **latency** and **RTT**. This module applies them to the classic satellite Internet design: **geostationary (GEO)** satellites. For decades, “satellite Internet” usually meant GEO. It works. It also feels slow for real-time apps — and that is mostly physics, not bad Wi‑Fi.

## Geostationary orbit in one picture

From the <InlineSource id="esaOrbits">ESA “Types of orbits”</InlineSource> page:

- GEO satellites fly above the **equator**.  
- They orbit in the same direction as Earth rotates, with a period of one **sidereal day** (~23 h 56 m).  
- Altitude is about **35,786 km**.  
- Orbital speed is on the order of **~3 km/s** (much slower than LEO).  
- From the ground, the satellite appears **fixed** in the sky — so a dish can stay pointed without tracking a racing target.

That “fixed in the sky” property is GEO’s superpower for broadcast TV and for simple terminals: aim once, stay locked.

## Coverage geometry: few satellites, huge footprints

Because GEO is so high, each satellite can see a **vast** region of Earth. ESA notes that a handful of GEO satellites can cover most of the populated world. That is the opposite of LEO, where each satellite’s service region is smaller and temporary for any given user.

So GEO broadband often looks operationally simple: a few expensive satellites, large coverage, stationary antennas.

## The bill comes due as delay

Radio waves travel at (approximately) the speed of light. Distance becomes time.

### Light-time sketch (order of magnitude)

At GEO altitude $h \approx 35{,}786$ km, a signal straight up to a satellite overhead and back down to Earth travels roughly $2h$ for a single “up and down” hop:

$$
t_{\text{up+down}} \approx \frac{2 \times 35{,}786\ \text{km}}{300{,}000\ \text{km/s}} \approx 0.24\ \text{s} = 240\ \text{ms}
$$

That ~**240 ms** figure is the classic pure-propagation estimate for a nadir GEO hop (see technical notes such as <InlineSource id="satsigLatency" />). Real user experience is often **worse**:

- the satellite is rarely perfectly overhead,  
- the path may be user → satellite → **gateway** → Internet (and back),  
- coding, modulation, and queueing add delay,  
- TCP and application protocols may need multiple RTTs.

Industry and educational comparisons commonly place **GEO broadband RTT in the ~500–700+ ms** experience band for interactive traffic. Starlink’s technology messaging contrasts LEO service with traditional satellite Internet on latency grounds (<InlineSource id="starlinkTech" />).

<Callout title="Connect to Module 1" variant="key">
If a video call needs frequent back-and-forth, a half-second RTT means every “your turn / my turn” moment is padded by physics. Bandwidth can still be high (you can download large files), but **conversation and competitive gaming feel the RTT**.
</Callout>

## Why “more bandwidth” does not fix GEO latency

Buying a thicker hose (bandwidth) does not shorten the hose (distance). Latency is dominated by **path length** and processing. GEO’s path length is locked by orbital mechanics: to stay fixed over a point on the equator with a 24-hour-class period, you must be near that altitude.

## GEO still matters

Be fair to GEO:

- Excellent for **broadcast** and for applications that tolerate delay.  
- Mature industry and wide coverage with few vehicles.  
- Simple antenna pointing for fixed installations.

LEO is not “GEO but better at everything.” LEO wins **latency** and can enable different terminal designs — at the cost of a far more complex space segment (Modules 3–7).

## Bridge to LEO

If delay is mostly distance, the engineering temptation is obvious: **fly lower**. Module 3 shows what that buys you — and the new problem it creates: satellites that race across the sky in minutes, not hours.

<Advanced title="Optional: double-hop mental model">
Many classic GEO Internet architectures are “bent-pipe”: the satellite relays user traffic to a ground gateway. A full interactive exchange can involve multiple legs (user↔sat↔gateway↔Internet). Light-time adds on each radio leg; terrestrial legs add fiber delay. That is why observed GEO RTTs often exceed the pure 240 ms up-down estimate.
