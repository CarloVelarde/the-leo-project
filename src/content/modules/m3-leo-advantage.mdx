# Low Earth Orbit: Why Closer Changes Everything

GEO sits ~36,000 km up. **Low Earth orbit (LEO)** sits hundreds of kilometers up. That single change reshapes latency, terminal design, and network architecture.

## What “LEO” means (authoritative framing)

According to the <InlineSource id="esaOrbits">ESA Types of orbits</InlineSource> page:

- LEO is **relatively close to Earth**, conventionally **below ~2,000 km** altitude (an upper bound related to the harsh environment of the Van Allen belts above).  
- Satellites generally stay **above ~180 km** because the atmosphere becomes too dense lower down.  
- Typical LEO motion: about **7.8 km/s** relative to Earth, completing an orbit in roughly **~90 minutes** (the ISS is a familiar example, circling Earth many times per day).

You will also see **MEO** (medium Earth orbit) between LEO and GEO — navigation systems like Galileo live there. For Starlink-style broadband, the action is LEO.

## Latency: the obvious win

Propagation delay scales with path length. If a satellite is ~50–100× closer than GEO, the pure light-time to the spacecraft drops from hundreds of milliseconds toward **a few milliseconds** on the space hop (exact numbers depend on elevation and routing through gateways or inter-satellite links).

That is the core of LEO broadband’s pitch: interactive applications become plausible again. Official Starlink technology materials emphasize lower latency relative to traditional satellite Internet and the LEO architecture that enables it (<InlineSource id="starlinkTech" />).

<Callout title="Still not zero latency" variant="note">
Even LEO must pay for: radio processing, queueing, path through gateways or space lasers, and the terrestrial Internet beyond. LEO removes the GEO *distance tax*; it does not repeal networking.
</Callout>

## Link budget intuition (no RF exam required)

Closer also helps the **link**: received signal strength falls off with distance. Lower altitude can mean stronger signals for a given power, or more margin for weather and antenna design. That helps enable **flat user terminals** with electronic steering (Module 6) rather than huge GEO dishes — though the engineering is still sophisticated.

## The continuous-coverage problem

Here is the trade that defines everything after this module.

A GEO sat can linger over a continent for years (from the user’s perspective: forever). A LEO sat:

- moves at ~7–8 km/s,  
- crosses a user’s sky in minutes,  
- and then is gone over the horizon.

ESA states the consequence plainly: communications satellites in LEO **often work as part of a constellation** — a coordinated group providing continuous coverage like a net around Earth (<InlineSource id="esaOrbits" />).

One satellite → temporary coverage.  
Many coordinated satellites → continuous service *if* design and handovers work.

## Why “just one LEO sat over my house” fails

Even if a sat passes over you every 90 minutes, you are offline most of the orbit. Broadband Internet requires **always-on** (or nearly always-on) connectivity. That forces:

1. **Many satellites** in view of Earth at once,  
2. arranged in **planes and shells** (Module 5),  
3. with **handovers** as satellites rise and set (Module 6),  
4. and a plan for traffic after it leaves your terminal (Module 7).

<Advanced title="Optional: period formula (circular orbit)">
For a circular orbit with semi-major axis $a = R_E + h$ and Earth gravitational parameter $\mu$:

$$
T = 2\pi \sqrt{\frac{a^3}{\mu}}
$$

Higher $h$ → larger $a$ → longer period and (usually) larger geometric footprint, but more light-time. The lab insight panel uses this model under documented assumptions.
</Advanced>
